# GUIDE — ADAPTING THIS CONSTITUTION

## Section 1 — Purpose of This Guide

This Constitution was written for Concordia, an imagined country. Much of it would work anywhere: the rights, the strict test for exceptions (I.4.c), the custody rules, the independent Election Authority, the limits on emergencies. But some of it assumes facts about Concordia that a real country may not share: that it is a federal union, that it follows the common law, that it has no indigenous peoples, and that it can afford what the Constitution promises.

This guide marks those parts. For each one it says what the text assumes, where it is, what a country adopting it would need to decide, and the main options.

It is not legal advice. A real adoption needs constitutional lawyers from the adopting country, translators, and, above all, the people who will live under it (Section 15).

## Section 2 — Decide Before You Adopt

Read this Section first. The Constitution is built to be hard to weaken, so some choices are close to permanent once it takes effect:

- **Rights:** No ordinary amendment may narrow a right in Articles I, II, III, or X, or lengthen a time limit that protects one (IX.3.a). A right can be tightened only by a Constitutional Review Convention, which can be called at most once every twenty-five years (IX.3.f), and some rights can never be narrowed at all.
- **Structure:** No amendment, by any route, may change the number of Supreme Court judges or judicial circuits, redraw a circuit, lengthen a term of office, or weaken the emergency limits or the independence of elections (IX.3.e).

An amendment may always *add* to a right. So the safe direction is to adopt the narrower version of any right you are unsure about, and widen it later if the country wants to. Every item in Section 9 is in this category.

## Section 4 — Federal or Unitary

**What it assumes:** Concordia is a federal union of states, territories, and a capital district, called constituent jurisdictions (VII.2.a). The states keep every power not given to the federal government (VII.3.a).

**Where this runs through the text:**

| Feature | Where |
|---|---|
| Two Senators for every constituent jurisdiction, whatever its population | IV.3.a, III.1.c |
| At least one Representative for every constituent jurisdiction | VII.2.b |
| Ratification by two-thirds of the jurisdictions holding a majority of the people | VIII.2.b |
| Amendments approved nationally *and* in a majority of jurisdictions | IX.2.b |
| Supreme Court circuits built from whole jurisdictions | VI.2.j |
| Congress's limited, listed powers | IV.1.b |
| Single-subject rule for state and local laws | VII.3.e |

**What to decide:** Is the country a federation, or a unitary state with one central government?

**Options for a unitary state:**

1. Replace constituent jurisdictions with regions that elect Senators but have only the powers the national law gives them.
2. Drop the Senate and keep a single chamber. Then rewrite every provision that needs a Senate vote: confirmations (V.4.a), treaties (V.7.b), impeachment trials (IV.9.d), emergency extensions (V.6.b), and the protective quorum (IV.4.b).
3. Ratify and amend by a single national majority, perhaps a supermajority, in place of the double majority in VIII.2.b and IX.2.b.
4. Replace the list of federal powers in IV.1.b with a general legislative power, still subject to Article I and IV.1.c.

## Section 5 — The Supreme Court and Its Circuits

**What it assumes:** Each of nine circuits elects one Supreme Court judge (VI.2.a). The circuits are drawn once, by land area, and can never be redrawn (VI.2.j). This stops anyone from gerrymandering the Court. The unequal populations of the circuits are an express exception to equal suffrage (III.1.c).

**The risk:** In a country where most people live in a few cities and the interior is nearly empty, a small population could elect as many judges as a huge one. Since IX.3.e forbids changing the circuits or the number of judges by any amendment, this cannot be fixed later.

**Options:**

1. **Keep it,** if the country's population is spread fairly evenly.
2. **Draw the circuits once by population,** then fix them forever as the text does now. This still prevents gerrymandering, because the lines never move, but drifts out of balance as people move.
3. **Elect judges nationally,** one seat at each general election, so every vote counts equally. This removes circuits entirely, and with them the protection against regional domination of the Court.
4. **Change the number of judges or circuits** to fit the country's size. Whatever number is chosen is fixed forever.

## Section 6 — Legal Tradition

**What it assumes:** A common-law system, like that of the United States or England.

| Feature | Where | Common-law assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Jury trial for offenses carrying more than six months | II.4.j | Lay juries decide guilt |
| Confronting and questioning witnesses | II.4.e | An adversarial trial, with each side presenting its case |
| Excluding unlawfully obtained evidence | II.9.c | A strict exclusionary rule |
| Binding Supreme Court decisions | VI.3.b | Precedent binds every court |
| A licence to practice law | VI.2.c(3) | A single legal profession |

**For a civil-law country** (most of Europe and Latin America, and much of Asia and Africa):

1. **Juries:** Many civil-law systems use mixed panels of professional and lay judges. II.4.j could allow a panel with a lay majority, deciding unanimously, in place of a jury.
2. **Investigating judges:** Where a judge, not the police, leads investigations, the custody rules in Article II, Sections 2 and 3 still apply, but the text should name who may order each step.
3. **Precedent:** Decide whether VI.3.b's binding force applies to every decision of the Supreme Court or only to its rulings on the Constitution.

**For a country with religious or customary law:** No religion may be established (I.1.b), and every law and court is subject to the Constitution (VIII.1.a). Customary or religious tribunals may still settle disputes between people who freely choose them, but every right in Article II applies to any tribunal that can punish, hold, or take property from anyone. Write that rule in expressly, rather than leaving it to the courts.

## Section 7 — Indigenous Peoples and National Minorities

**What it assumes:** Nothing. The Constitution protects individuals equally (I.4.a), protects every person's language (I.1.f), and allows remedies for specific discrimination (I.4.a). It has no provision for peoples who lived on the land before the state, or for national minorities as groups.

**Why it needs an express provision:** Equal protection forbids distinctions based on ethnicity or national origin unless an express permission covers them (I.4.a, I.4.c). Group rights for indigenous peoples would therefore be struck down unless the Constitution itself provides for them.

**Options, which can be combined:**

1. **Self-government:** recognized indigenous nations govern their own internal affairs, subject to Articles I, II, III, and X for every person within them.
2. **Land:** traditional lands are recognized, and taking or using them requires the consent of the people concerned, or just compensation at the least (II.1.c).
3. **Consultation:** a law or project that directly affects an indigenous people requires their free, prior, and informed consent, or at least genuine consultation.
4. **Representation:** reserved seats in Congress, or an advisory body with a right to be heard.
5. **Languages:** recognition of indigenous or minority languages as official in the areas where they are spoken (I.1.f already allows official languages by law).

Each option must be written as an express permission for purposes of I.4.c, and should say whom it covers and who decides membership.

## Section 8 — Citizenship

| Rule | Where | What to decide |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone born in the country is a citizen | VII.1.a | Most countries in the Americas do this. Most of Europe and Asia do not |
| A child born abroad to a citizen parent is a citizen, with no limit on generations | VII.1.a | Many countries stop after one or two generations abroad, unless the child would be stateless |
| No one may lose citizenship, except by renouncing it or for fraud in naturalization | VII.1.d | Whether to keep this firm rule |
| Citizens abroad vote where they or a parent last lived | VII.2.c | Whether to give citizens abroad their own representatives instead |
| No language test for naturalization | VII.1.b | Whether to require any official language |
| Dual citizenship | Not addressed | Whether citizens may hold another citizenship. Silence means they may |

Citizenship rules sit in Article VII, so they can be amended under Article IX. They are still worth getting right at the start.

## Section 9 — Value Choices That Are Locked In

Each of these is a right under Articles I, II, III, or X, so it cannot be narrowed by ordinary amendment once adopted (Section 2). A country should decide each one deliberately.

| Choice | Where | What the text does now | A narrower alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal use of substances | I.3.b | Adults may possess and use any substance; minors face no criminal penalty | Protect only specific substances, or leave it to ordinary law |
| Abortion | I.3.a | A decision for the pregnant person, with no time limit stated; for a minor at every age | Set a time limit after which it is allowed only for health reasons |
| Medical assistance in dying | I.3.c | Allowed for competent adults with serious incurable conditions | Limit to a person whose death is reasonably foreseeable, or leave to ordinary law |
| Plural marriage | I.1.j | Allowed, with the consent of every spouse and equal rules for both sexes | Marriage of two persons |
| Risks to oneself | I.3.a | Safety laws only by warnings, safety gear, prescriptions, and product standards, enforced by fines | Allow any law that satisfies I.4.c |
| Arms | Article X | An individual right with a licence to purchase, prohibited persons, and places | A narrower right, or none |
| Capital punishment | II.7.e | Allowed only for aggravated murder, with heavy safeguards | Abolished. An amendment may abolish it later, because abolition widens a right |
| Lockdowns and quarantine | I.1.g | No general stay-at-home orders; borders and non-essential services may close for thirty days at a time in a declared disease emergency | Allow broader measures, subject to I.4.c |
| Campaign finance | III.10 | Organizations may not contribute; public matching funds | A different system |
| Self-defense, and the right of resistance | I.3.a, X.4 | A right of every person, and a last-resort right of citizens to restore the constitutional order | Narrower or no right of resistance |

## Section 11 — Languages

The Constitution sets no official language, and lets a law choose one or more (I.1.f). It protects every person's use of their own language at work, in shops, and before the government, and forbids a language test for naturalization (VII.1.b).

**What to decide:** The authoritative language or languages of the Constitution itself. Where it is adopted in more than one language, say which text controls if translations differ, or that all are equally authoritative and a court reconciles them.

## Section 12 — What It Costs

The Constitution promises things that cost money every year. A country should estimate each one before adopting it, because most are rights that cannot later be cut.

| Promise | Where |
|---|---|
| Free primary and secondary education; higher education free or with enough aid that no one is excluded | I.2.a |
| Compensation for businesses and workers closed by a disease order | I.1.g |
| Support and pay for anyone in isolation or quarantine | I.1.g |
| Minimum wage for labor in a criminal sentence | I.1.d |
| Counsel for the accused and on appeal | II.4.a, II.4.h |
| Counsel in civil cases about homes, children, liberty, or removal | II.1.e |
| Compensation for victims of violent crime | II.1.f |
| Palliative care wherever health care is provided or funded | I.3.c |
| Court review of detention within 48 hours, at any hour, and electronic warrants day and night | II.3.c, II.6.d |
| Trial within 180 days for anyone held in custody | II.4.d |
| The Election Authority, census, districting commission, public campaign financing, Judicial Conduct Commission, Judicial Enforcement Service, central bank, and Auditor-General | III.3, VII.2.b, III.4.c, III.10.e, VI.4.d, VI.3.e, IV.10.f, IV.10.g |
| Free remote schooling, with devices, if schools close | I.1.g |

The bills and the education and economic acts are ordinary laws. They are not part of the Constitution, and a country adopting the Constitution does not adopt them. Its first Congress may enact, change, or ignore each one (IV.1.d).

## Section 13 — International Obligations

- **Treaties are below the Constitution** (V.7.b). A treaty that conflicts with it has no effect in domestic law. Check every treaty the country has signed for conflicts before adoption, especially human-rights treaties.
- **Treaties signed by the constituent jurisdictions** lapse after one year unless Concordia adopts them (VIII.3.e).
- **Membership in a supranational union,** whose law takes priority over national law, is not provided for. A country that wants to join one needs an express provision allowing it, with limits.
- **The death penalty** (II.7.e) may conflict with treaties some countries have signed that abolish it.
- **Refugees:** No one may be sent to a place where they face torture or persecution (II.2.p). This matches the Refugee Convention and the Convention against Torture.

## Section 14 — The Founding Timeline

Article VIII sets a demanding schedule. The young state has to do all of this while also governing:

| When | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 | The Constitution takes effect; the Founding Convention becomes the interim government | VIII.2.b, VIII.3.a |
| Day 0 | Every person in custody becomes entitled to the protections of Article II | VIII.3.f |
| Within 60 days | First random drawings of the Election Authority and the districting commission | VIII.3.b |
| Within 90 days | Court review for everyone already in custody | VIII.3.f |
| Within 180 days | First House districts; assignment of jurisdictions to judicial circuits | VIII.3.b |
| Within 1 year | First general election for every office, including all nine Supreme Court judges | VIII.3.c |
| Within 1 year | Each jurisdiction publishes its list of unrelated provisions in inherited law | VIII.3.e |
| 1 year | Treaties of the constituent jurisdictions lapse unless adopted | VIII.3.e |
| About 14 months | The first Congress and President take office, 75 days after the election | VIII.3.c |
| 90 days after Congress convenes | Every law of the interim government expires unless re-enacted | VIII.3.a |
| 1 year after Congress convenes | Judicial Conduct Commission selected | VIII.3.b |
| 2 years after Congress convenes | Unrelated provisions of inherited law lose effect unless re-enacted | VIII.3.e |
| Within 5 years | First census | VII.2.b |
| 25 years | First vote on whether to call a Constitutional Review Convention | IX.3.f |

**What to check:** whether the country's courts can review every detention within 48 hours from day one; whether it has enough voters applying for the random drawings; and whether its legislatures can review their whole body of law in time. Where the answer is no, lengthen the period *before* adoption. A transition period that is too short becomes a broken promise on the first day.

## Section 15 — How to Adopt It

1. **Involve the people.** Constitutions that last are usually written with wide public participation: hearings, consultations, and a drafting body that reflects the country. A finished text adopted from outside carries less authority, however good it is. Treat this text as a starting draft, not a final one.
2. **Make the decisions in Sections 4 through 13.** Record each one, and the reasons, so that later courts can understand the choices.
3. **Review it legally.** Have constitutional lawyers from the adopting country check every provision against the country's law, institutions, and treaties.
4. **Translate it carefully.** Decide which text controls (Section 11).
5. **Test it.** Before adoption, apply it to real past events, such as an election dispute, an emergency, or a contested arrest, and see whether it gives clear answers. The cases in this collection show the method.
6. **Run the citation check.** After every change, run `python3 scripts/check_refs.py` to confirm that every cross-reference still points to a provision that exists.
7. **Ratify it** by referendum in each jurisdiction (VIII.2).
