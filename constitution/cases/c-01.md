# CASE — OKAFOR v. CITY OF WESTBROOK

**Court:** Supreme Court of Concordia, on appeal from the Court of Appeals

**Question:** Whether a city may forbid a homeowner from building a solid fence that shields the yard of a home from view

**Result:** For the homeowner, in part. Decided 7–2.

**Provisions applied:** II.6.a, II.6.b, II.6.c, I.4.c, VI.3.c, VI.3.d, II.9.c

---

## Summary

The City of Westbrook limited every fence beside or behind a house to four feet in height, and required at least half of every such fence to be open to view. The City said the rule kept the neighborhood looking open, deterred crime by letting neighbors and passing officers see into yards, and let code inspectors check yards without entering them.

The Court holds that the yard immediately around a home is part of the home under II.6.a, and that a law requiring a homeowner to keep that yard open to view, so that officials can watch it, is a governmental intrusion on the privacy of the home. Such a law must satisfy the constitutional standard for exceptions in I.4.c. The City's rule did not: appearance is not a compelling interest, the City offered no evidence that open fences prevent crime, and letting officials look into yards without a warrant is not a lawful purpose at all.

The Court upholds the part of the ordinance that keeps fences low near street corners and driveways, where a tall fence would block drivers' view of pedestrians, because that rule directly protects the life and physical safety of others.

---

## Background

### The ordinance

Section 14-210 of the Westbrook Municipal Code provided:

1. No fence in a side or rear yard of a residential lot shall exceed four feet in height.
2. At least fifty percent of the surface of every such fence shall be open, so that the yard behind it is visible from adjoining property and the street.
3. No fence, wall, or hedge within a sight triangle shall exceed three feet in height. A **sight triangle** is the area within twenty-five feet of the corner of two streets, or within ten feet of the edge of a driveway where it meets a sidewalk.
4. A violation is subject to a fine of one hundred dollars for each day it continues.

The preamble of the ordinance stated three purposes: to "preserve the open and welcoming character of Westbrook's neighborhoods"; to promote "natural surveillance," so that "neighbors and patrol officers can observe activity in yards and deter crime"; and to "allow code enforcement officers to verify compliance from the public way without the need for entry onto private property."

### The fence

Adaeze Okafor owns a single-family house on an interior lot in Westbrook. Her rear yard adjoins a public footpath and a parking lot. After strangers repeatedly photographed her children playing in the yard, she built a solid wooden fence six feet high along the rear and side lines of her lot. No part of the fence stands within a sight triangle.

The City cited her under Section 14-210, items 1 and 2, and imposed daily fines. By the time of trial, the fines exceeded nine thousand dollars. A code enforcement officer testified that he could no longer "see what is going on back there," and that before the fence he had used his view from the footpath to identify "unpermitted sheds, unregistered vehicles, and other conditions" in neighboring yards.

### Proceedings below

Okafor sued the City in the trial court, claiming that items 1 and 2 of the ordinance violate her right to privacy in her home under II.6.a. Her neighbor, Desmond Pryce, joined the case to support the City, stating that the fence blocked his view across her yard toward a park and made the neighborhood feel "closed off."

The trial court upheld the ordinance, reasoning that the right to privacy concerns searches by officials, not the height of fences. The Court of Appeals reversed. It held that the yard of a home is protected by II.6.a and that the City had not justified its rule under I.4.c. The City appealed.

---

## Questions Presented

1. Is the yard immediately surrounding a home part of the "home" protected by II.6.a?
2. Is a law that requires a homeowner to keep that yard open to view a "governmental intrusion" on privacy, so that it must satisfy I.4.c?
3. If so, does Section 14-210 satisfy I.4.c?
4. Does a neighbor's wish for an open view justify the rule?

---

## Opinion of the Court

*Judge Halvorsen delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Judges Mbeki, Castellanos, Ruiz-Tanaka, Lindqvist, and Achterberg, and by Judge Oyelaran as to Parts 1, 4, 5, and 6.*

### 1. The yard of a home is part of the home

II.6.a protects every person's privacy "in their person, home, effects, communications, and personal information." The Constitution does not define "home." We give the word its ordinary meaning, and where a provision can reasonably be read more than one way we must adopt the reading that preserves the greater protection of the right (VI.3.d; I.4.c).

A home is not only the space inside its walls. The yard immediately around a house is where a family's private life continues out of doors: where children play, where people rest, garden, and gather. Its use is bound up with the house itself, and it is commonly enclosed or set apart for that reason. We hold that the area immediately surrounding a dwelling and used as part of the household's private life is part of the home under II.6.a.

This does not mean that every part of a large property is a home. A distant field or an open pasture is not used as an extension of the household, and the protection of II.6.a for such land is that of property and effects rather than of the home. Okafor's side and rear yards, which adjoin her house and in which her children play, are part of her home.

### 2. A rule that keeps the home open to watching is a governmental intrusion

The City argues that II.6.a restrains only searches, and that a fence rule searches nothing. We disagree.

II.6.a defines a **governmental intrusion** as "governmental access, examination, monitoring, acquisition, or interference with a protected person, place, thing, communication, or information." The definition is not limited to searches, which II.6.b governs separately. It reaches monitoring, and it reaches interference.

Section 14-210 does both. Its own preamble states that it exists so that "patrol officers can observe activity in yards" and so that inspectors can "verify compliance from the public way without the need for entry onto private property." A law that requires a person to keep the private spaces of the home exposed, for the stated purpose that officials may watch them, is a form of governmental monitoring of the home. It also interferes with the home directly, by forbidding the homeowner to take the most ordinary step to protect it from view.

The City relies on the rule that an officer standing in a public place may note what anyone could see from there. Whatever the scope of that rule, it describes what an officer may do when a person has left something open to view. It does not permit the government to require the person to leave it open. If it did, the government could abolish the privacy of every yard, and then of every window, by forbidding fences and curtains. The protection of II.6.a does not depend on what the government chooses to leave visible.

Because Section 14-210, items 1 and 2, intrudes on a constitutional right, the City bears the burden of justifying it under every requirement of I.4.c.

### 3. The City's reasons do not satisfy I.4.c

A right may be restricted only where the Constitution expressly permits it or where the restriction is necessary to protect the fundamental rights of another person (I.4.c). No provision of the Constitution expressly permits restrictions on the privacy of the home for the purposes the City states. We therefore ask whether the rule directly prevents an actual or imminent injury to another person's fundamental rights, and if so whether it is narrowly tailored, strictly necessary, the least restrictive means, and proportional.

**Neighborhood character.** An open and welcoming appearance may be a pleasant civic aim, but it is not the protection of anyone's fundamental right, and it is not a compelling governmental interest. I.4.c excludes "offense, disagreement, disapproval of another person's lawful choices." A preference for how a neighbor's yard should look is exactly that.

**Crime deterrence.** The protection of life and physical safety is a compelling interest, and the City is right that crime threatens it. But I.4.c requires the City to show that its rule directly prevents an actual or imminent harm, not a "general, remote, or speculative risk." The City offered no evidence that open fences reduce crime in Westbrook or anywhere else. Its witness agreed that solid fences are common in neighboring cities without any measured difference in crime. And the rule is not narrowly tailored: it applies to every residential yard in the City, regardless of any history of crime, and less restrictive measures such as street lighting, patrols, and locks on gates would protect safety without stripping the home of privacy. Okafor, for her part, built her fence to protect her children from being watched by strangers. A rule that forbids her to do so in the name of safety does not protect the physical safety of anyone she has been shown to threaten.

**Inspection without entry.** The City's third purpose is not a lawful purpose at all. A governmental examination of the home for evidence of a violation is a search under II.6.b, and it requires a warrant unless the Constitution expressly authorizes it or obtaining a warrant is not reasonably practicable (II.6.c). The City may not avoid that requirement by legislating that homes be kept open to inspection. A purpose that the Constitution forbids cannot serve as the compelling interest that I.4.c requires.

Items 1 and 2 of Section 14-210, as applied to side and rear yards, therefore violate II.6.a.

### 4. The sight-triangle rule is valid

Item 3 stands on different ground. A fence, wall, or hedge near a street corner or at the edge of a driveway can hide a child on the sidewalk from a driver backing out, or hide one car from another at an intersection. The record contains traffic engineering evidence, which Okafor did not dispute, that the three-foot limit within twenty-five feet of a corner and ten feet of a driveway is the smallest restriction that preserves the sight lines needed to avoid collisions.

That rule directly prevents an actual risk of injury to the life and physical safety of identifiable classes of people, namely pedestrians and drivers at those places. It is confined to the small areas where the risk exists, and its burden on privacy is slight. It satisfies I.4.c. It does not apply to Okafor's fence, which stands outside every sight triangle.

Our holding does not prevent a city from adopting other rules for fences that satisfy I.4.c, such as structural standards that keep a fence from falling on a neighbor, rules that keep emergency access to a building open, or a maximum height justified by evidence of a danger to physical safety. Each must be justified on its own record.

### 5. The neighbor's view

Pryce argues that the fence harms him by blocking his view toward the park and by making the neighborhood feel closed. The Constitution protects many things, but it does not give one person a right to see into or across another person's home. His objection is a disagreement with, and disapproval of, his neighbor's lawful choice, which I.4.c says is not harm to another person's rights. Nothing in this decision affects any easement or other property right that a neighbor may hold by agreement or by law, and Pryce claims none.

### 6. Remedy

A law held unconstitutional has no legal effect to the extent of the violation, and the remainder is given effect where it can operate independently (VI.3.c). Items 1 and 2 of Section 14-210 have no legal effect as applied to the side and rear yards of a home. Item 3 and the remainder of the ordinance remain in force.

Okafor is entitled to an effective remedy for the violation (II.9.c). The City shall refund every fine it has collected from her under items 1 and 2, cancel every fine it has imposed but not collected, and is permanently enjoined from enforcing items 1 and 2 against her fence.

---

## Disposition

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed in part. Items 1 and 2 of Section 14-210 of the Westbrook Municipal Code violate II.6.a as applied to the side and rear yards of a home and have no legal effect to that extent. Item 3 is valid. The case is returned to the trial court to enter judgment for Okafor and to order the refund and cancellation of fines.

---

## Concurring Opinion

*Judge Oyelaran, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.*

I join Parts 1, 4, 5, and 6 of the Court's opinion, and I agree that items 1 and 2 cannot stand. My reason is narrower than the Court's. The ordinance states that it exists so that inspectors can verify compliance "without the need for entry onto private property": that is, so that officials can examine homes without the warrant that II.6.c requires. A law enacted for a purpose the Constitution forbids is invalid on that ground alone, and I would rest the judgment there. I would leave for a future case whether a rule limiting fence height, adopted for a lawful purpose and supported by evidence, intrudes on privacy at all. That question is harder than the Court allows, and this record does not require us to answer it.

## Dissenting Opinion

*Judge Brandt, joined by Judge Seo, dissenting.*

The Court today turns a rule about fences into a rule about searches. II.6.a protects persons from "unjustified governmental intrusion into a person's protected personal sphere." No official entered Okafor's yard, examined her papers, or watched her through her windows. The City told her how tall a structure she may build on her land, as cities have always done.

The Court reasons that because the ordinance's preamble spoke of observation, the ordinance is a form of monitoring. But the City's motives cannot change what the ordinance does, which is regulate the height and design of structures. If the City had said nothing of officers, the Court's reasoning would collapse. A rule about building height does not become a search because a council member hoped it would make policing easier, and the remedy for an unlawful search by an inspector is suppression or damages in the case where it happens, not the invalidation of a building code.

Nor is the Court's line between fences and other building rules a stable one. Every rule limiting the height of a building, the placement of a hedge, or the size of a window affects how much of a home others can see. The Court assures us that cities may still adopt rules that "satisfy I.4.c," but under its reasoning every such rule must now pass the strictest test in the Constitution, with the city bearing the burden. That standard was written for real intrusions on rights, not for the ordinary business of a planning office.

I would hold that a generally applicable limit on the height or design of a structure is not a governmental intrusion under II.6.a, and that an inspector's use of any view the limit leaves open is governed by II.6.b and II.6.c in the case where it occurs. I respectfully dissent.
