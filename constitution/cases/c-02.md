# CASE — SORENSEN v. REGISTRAR OF MARRIAGES OF KESTREL

*Decided together with* **Calder v. Calder**

**Court:** Supreme Court of Concordia, on appeal from the Court of Appeals

**Questions:** Whether a state may register plural marriages only where a husband adds a wife; whether a state may require that each existing spouse consent privately before a registrar, and then wait thirty days; and whether a spouse's written consent to a new spouse is freely given when it was obtained by threats

**Result:** For the Sorensens on the first question and in part on the second; for Ruth Calder on the third. Decided 9–0 on the first question; 9–0 upholding the private interview and 6–3 striking the waiting period; and 6–3 on the third question.

**Provisions applied:** I.1.j, I.4.a, I.4.c, VIII.3.e, VI.3.c, VI.3.d, II.9.c

---

## Summary

The Constitution permits an adult to marry one or more consenting adults. No person may join an existing marriage without the informed, written consent of every existing spouse, given freely. The law of plural marriage must apply equally whatever the sex of each spouse, and no law or practice may allow only persons of one sex to have more than one spouse (I.1.j).

The State of Kestrel registered plural marriages under a statute, older than the Constitution, that allowed only a husband to add a wife. After the Constitution took effect, Kestrel kept that statute and added two requirements: each existing spouse must consent in a private interview with a registrar, and the new marriage may not be registered until thirty days after the last interview.

The Court holds that the husband-only rule violates I.1.j, and that the remedy is to extend the statute to every spouse, not to abolish plural marriage in Kestrel. It upholds the private interview, because it is the least restrictive way to make sure that each existing spouse's consent is the person's own. It strikes down the thirty-day wait, because Kestrel offered no evidence that it protects anyone once the interview has been held.

In the second case, the Court holds that a spouse's consent is not freely given when it is obtained by a threat of violence, a threat to do something unlawful, or an abuse of authority. A spouse's statement that they will leave the marriage is not such a threat, because leaving is that spouse's right. A threat to stop paying support that the law requires is. Because Ruth Calder's consent was obtained by such a threat, her husband's later marriage to Priya Nair has no legal effect. The Court holds that Nair, who acted in good faith, may not be penalized, and that her child has every right of any other child.

## Background

### The Kestrel statutes

Section 12 of the Kestrel Domestic Relations Code was enacted before the Constitution took effect. It provides:

> A husband may, with the written consent of his wife or wives, take an additional wife. The registrar shall record the marriage and the names of all wives.

Kestrel did not repeal Section 12 when the Constitution took effect. Instead, the legislature added Section 12A:

> 1. Before recording an additional marriage under Section 12, the registrar shall interview each existing spouse in person, privately, outside the presence of every other spouse and of the person to be married. The registrar shall confirm that the spouse understands the effect of the marriage, consents to it, and has not been threatened or pressured, and shall record the consent in writing.
> 2. The registrar shall not record the additional marriage until thirty days after the last interview under item 1.

The registrar's office read Section 12 as written, and recorded additional marriages only where a husband added a wife.

### The Sorensens

Ines Sorensen and Tomas Sorensen have been married for eleven years. Ines and Kofi Mensah wish to marry, and Tomas consents. All three went to the registrar together. The registrar refused to begin the interviews, on the ground that Section 12 allows only a husband to take an additional spouse. The registrar added that, even if Section 12 allowed the marriage, Section 12A would require private interviews and then a thirty-day wait.

Ines Sorensen, Tomas Sorensen, and Mensah sued the registrar. They claim that Section 12 violates I.1.j by allowing only husbands to add spouses, and that Section 12A violates I.1.j and I.4.c by burdening the right to marry. They object to the private interview because it treats them as suspects, and to the wait because it delays their marriage for no reason.

### The Calders

Ruth Calder and Daniel Calder have been married for fourteen years and have three children. Ruth does not work outside the home. The family's only income is Daniel's, and the house is held in his name.

Before Section 12A took effect, Daniel asked Ruth to consent to his marriage to Priya Nair. Ruth refused. The trial court found, on testimony it believed, that Daniel then told Ruth two things: that if she did not consent he would leave her, and that if she did not consent he would stop paying the mortgage and the children's school fees, so that she and the children "would find out what it costs to live without me." Two days later Ruth signed a written consent, which was recorded with the marriage. Daniel and Nair married. Nair knew nothing of what Daniel had said to Ruth. Daniel and Nair have since had a child.

Eight months later, Ruth sued for a declaration that her consent was not freely given and that the marriage of Daniel and Nair has no legal effect. Nair was joined as a party.

### Proceedings below

In *Sorensen*, the trial court held that Section 12 violated I.1.j, but that it could not rewrite the statute. It declared Section 12 void, which left Kestrel with no law allowing any plural marriage, and upheld Section 12A. The Court of Appeals agreed that Section 12 was invalid but held that the proper remedy was to extend it to every spouse. It upheld the interview and struck down the wait. Kestrel and the Sorensens each appealed.

In *Calder*, the trial court held that Ruth's consent was not freely given and declared the marriage of Daniel and Nair void. The Court of Appeals affirmed. Daniel and Nair appealed.

The Court heard the two cases together because both turn on the consent of existing spouses under I.1.j.

---

## Questions Presented

1. Does a law that allows only a husband to add a spouse violate I.1.j, and if so, what is the remedy?
2. May a state require that each existing spouse consent in a private interview with a registrar? May it require a thirty-day wait after the interview?
3. When is a spouse's consent to a new spouse "given freely"? Was Ruth Calder's?
4. What is the effect of a marriage entered without an existing spouse's free consent, and what protection does the law give to the new spouse and to any child?

---

## Opinion of the Court

*Judge Mbeki delivered the opinion of the Court. Judges Halvorsen, Castellanos, Ruiz-Tanaka, Lindqvist, and Oyelaran joined the opinion in full. Judges Achterberg, Seo, and Brandt joined Parts 1, 2, and 6, and Part 3 except as to the waiting period.*

### 1. Section 12 violates I.1.j

I.1.j states that the law of plural marriage "shall apply equally whatever the sex of each spouse, and no law or practice shall allow only persons of one sex to have more than one spouse." Section 12 allows a husband to take an additional wife, and allows nothing else. It is exactly what I.1.j forbids. It also denies a right because of sex, a protected status, without any justification under I.4.c (I.4.a).

Kestrel argues that Section 12 is older than the Constitution, and that the Constitution therefore governs only marriages made under new laws. But the laws of each constituent jurisdiction remained in force when the Constitution took effect only "to the extent they are consistent with this Constitution." Any part of a law that is inconsistent "has no legal effect from the day this Constitution takes effect" (VIII.3.e). Section 12's limit to husbands has had no legal effect since that day.

### 2. The remedy is to extend Section 12, not to abolish it

The trial court treated the whole of Section 12 as void. That was error.

A law held unconstitutional has no legal effect "to the extent of the violation," and the remainder is given effect where it can operate independently (VI.3.c; VIII.3.e). The violation in Section 12 is not that it allows plural marriage. The Constitution itself grants that right (I.1.j). The violation is the limit to husbands and wives. When that limit falls away, what remains is a law that allows a spouse, with the written consent of every existing spouse, to take an additional spouse, and that directs the registrar to record the marriage and the names of all spouses. That law can operate on its own, and it is the law that I.1.j requires.

Kestrel answers that a court that extends a statute is writing law, which is the legislature's task. In most cases where a law favors one group unconstitutionally, a court must choose between extending the benefit and taking it from everyone, and the choice may belong to the legislature. Here the Constitution has made the choice. The right to marry more than one person belongs to every adult, and I.1.j requires that its law "apply equally." To strike Section 12 entirely would take from every person in Kestrel a right the Constitution grants, in order to cure a denial of that right to some. Where a law can be read and applied in more than one way, we must adopt the reading that preserves the greater protection of the right (VI.3.d). We therefore read Section 12, from the day the Constitution took effect, as applying to every spouse equally.

The Sorensens may marry Mensah on the terms that apply to every other plural marriage in Kestrel.

### 3. The private interview is valid; the thirty-day wait is not

A law that sets conditions on marrying restricts the right in I.1.j, and must satisfy I.4.c.

**The interview.** The Constitution itself requires that every existing spouse's consent be informed, written, "given freely," and "recorded with the marriage" (I.1.j). The State does not merely have a compelling interest in making sure that consent is free. It has a constitutional duty to record only consent that is. And the consent requirement exists to protect the rights of a particular person, the existing spouse, against a particular harm: being made part of a marriage they did not freely accept. That is harm to another person's rights under I.4.c.

The Sorensens argue that a signed form is enough. The record shows why it is not. A consent form signed at a kitchen table can be signed under the eyes of the person who wants it signed. *Calder* is such a case. A short interview with a neutral official, out of the presence of the other spouses and the new partner, gives each existing spouse one moment in which to say no without being watched. Kestrel showed that registrars ask a fixed set of questions, that the interview takes about twenty minutes, that it is offered on any working day, in the spouse's own language (I.1.f), and, for a spouse who cannot travel, by secure video. The Sorensens did not identify any measure that would protect a spouse's free choice as well with less burden. We hold that the interview satisfies I.4.c.

The Sorensens also say that the interview treats them as suspects. It does not. It is asked of every existing spouse in every plural marriage, whatever their sex, and it accuses no one. It is a precaution taken so that the consent the Constitution requires is real.

**The wait.** The thirty-day wait stands on different ground. Kestrel offered no evidence that a spouse who has consented freely in a private interview is helped by a further month's delay, or that any spouse has ever withdrawn consent during it. Its only witness said that the wait "gives people time to reflect." But a spouse who wishes to reflect may decline to consent until ready, and the interview already gives them a private chance to do so. A restriction that adds nothing to the protection the interview gives is not strictly necessary, and is not the least restrictive means (I.4.c).

Kestrel points out that the Constitution itself requires a ten-day wait before medical assistance in dying (I.3.c). That comparison does not help it. Where the drafters of the Constitution wanted a waiting period, they wrote one, and they wrote it for a decision that cannot be undone. They wrote none for marriage, which any spouse may leave at any time (I.1.j). Item 2 of Section 12A has no legal effect.

### 4. When consent is "given freely"

I.1.j requires that the consent of an existing spouse be "given freely," but does not define the phrase. The Consent paragraph of the same subsection says that no person shall be "forced or pressured into a marriage by violence, threat, or abuse of authority." We read the two together. A consent is not given freely if it was obtained by:

1. violence, or a threat of violence, against the spouse or anyone close to them;
2. a threat to do something that the person making it has no right to do; or
3. an abuse of authority, such as that of an employer, a landlord, an official, or a religious leader over the spouse.

A spouse who is told that the other will **leave the marriage** if consent is refused has not been threatened within this rule. Every spouse may end a marriage, or leave it without ending it for the others (I.1.j). A person who announces that they will exercise their own constitutional right does not coerce the person who hears it, however painful the choice that follows. If it were otherwise, every consent given to keep a marriage together would be open to challenge, and the right to leave would become a weapon against the person who exercised it.

A spouse who is told that the other will **stop paying support the law requires** stands differently. Daniel Calder had no right to abandon the support of his children, and the trial court found that the mortgage and the children's school fees were obligations he owed the household under Kestrel law. He did not say that he would leave and that the law would then settle what he owed (I.1.j). He said that, unless Ruth consented, he would make her and the children "find out what it costs to live without me." That is a threat to do what he had no right to do, made to a spouse who depended entirely on him, and it was made to obtain her consent. Her consent was not given freely.

A recorded written consent is presumed to be free. A spouse who challenges it bears the burden of proving the facts that show otherwise, under the ordinary civil standard. Ruth Calder met that burden. The trial court believed her testimony, and we see no reason to disturb that finding.

### 5. The effect on the Calder marriage, and the protection of Nair and her child

**The marriage.** I.1.j provides that "no person shall join an existing marriage" without the free consent of every existing spouse. The Consent paragraph of I.1.j says that "no marriage contrary to this paragraph has legal effect," but the plural-marriage paragraph does not say in so many words what follows when its rule is broken. We hold that the same consequence follows. A joining that the Constitution forbids cannot be a lawful joining. If it could, the requirement of free consent would protect no one: the spouse who was coerced would remain married, against their will, to the very arrangement they were coerced into. The marriage of Daniel Calder and Priya Nair has no legal effect.

This holding does not depend on what Nair knew. The right that was violated is Ruth's, and it is violated whether or not the new spouse knew how her consent was obtained.

**Nair.** Nair acted in good faith, and the Constitution does not permit her to be punished for another person's wrong. No penalty of any kind may be imposed on her because of the marriage, and nothing in this decision limits any claim she has against Daniel Calder under the general law of Kestrel, including for property she contributed and for support. Kestrel may by law give a person who entered a void marriage in good faith the property and support rights of a spouse, and we note that many legal systems do. The Constitution neither requires nor forbids it.

**The child.** "A child has the same rights whether or not the child's parents are married" (I.1.j). The child of Daniel Calder and Nair is Daniel's child in law, with every right to support, inheritance, and care that any of his children has. No part of this decision affects that child's relationship with either parent.

**Ruth.** Ruth Calder remains married to Daniel Calder unless either of them ends the marriage. She is entitled to an effective remedy for the violation of her right (II.9.c), which in this case is the declaration that the marriage of Daniel and Nair has no legal effect, and the correction of the marriage record so that it names no spouse whose joining was unlawful.

### 6. Time for challenge

The parties ask how long a spouse has to challenge a consent. The Constitution sets no period. A state may set a reasonable one by law, so long as it begins only when the coercion has ended, since a spouse who is still under threat cannot be expected to sue. Kestrel has set none, and Ruth Calder sued within eight months.

---

## Disposition

In *Sorensen v. Registrar of Marriages of Kestrel*, the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed. Section 12 of the Kestrel Domestic Relations Code applies equally to every spouse. Item 1 of Section 12A is valid. Item 2 of Section 12A has no legal effect. The registrar shall conduct the interviews required by item 1 and, on free consent, record the marriage of Ines Sorensen and Kofi Mensah.

In *Calder v. Calder*, the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed. The marriage of Daniel Calder and Priya Nair has no legal effect, and the marriage record shall be corrected. No penalty shall be imposed on Nair. The child of Daniel Calder and Nair has every right of any child of Daniel Calder. The case is returned to the trial court for any claims between Nair and Daniel Calder.

## Concurring Opinion

*Judge Oyelaran, concurring.*

I join the Court's opinion in full, and write only to say a word about the remedy in Part 2. Courts should be slow to extend a statute to people the legislature left out, because the legislature may prefer to end a benefit rather than widen it, and that choice is usually the legislature's. The Court does not depart from that rule today. It applies the rare exception: where the Constitution itself grants the right that the statute carries out, a court that strikes the whole statute does not return the choice to the legislature. It takes away a constitutional right. Kestrel remains free to enact a new law of plural marriage, so long as that law applies equally.

## Opinion Concurring in Part and Dissenting in Part

*Judge Achterberg, joined by Judge Seo, concurring in Parts 1, 2, and 6, concurring in part in Part 3, and dissenting from Parts 4 and 5.*

I agree that Section 12's limit to husbands is void, that the cure is to extend it, and that the private interview is valid. I would also uphold the thirty-day wait. A waiting period is the most familiar protection the law knows against decisions made under pressure, and Kestrel adopted it after this very case showed what pressure looks like. A month's delay in registering a marriage is a slight burden, and I would give the legislature room to choose it.

On the Calder appeal I part company with the Court. Its line between a threat to leave and a threat to stop paying support cannot be held in practice. A spouse who leaves a household usually stops paying for it, at least until a court orders otherwise. Every "I will leave" carries an unspoken "and you will have less." Under today's rule, whether a consent stands will depend on whether the departing spouse said the second part aloud.

And the Court's remedy falls on the one person who did nothing wrong. Priya Nair married in good faith, on a consent recorded by the State. She has a child. Today she learns that she was never married. I would hold that a consent obtained by an unlawful threat makes the new marriage voidable at the election of the coerced spouse, from the date of the judgment forward, leaving intact the rights that Nair and her child acquired in the meantime. That protects Ruth Calder's right fully, since she would no longer be part of a marriage she did not freely accept, without treating Nair as though she had never been a wife. I respectfully dissent from Parts 4 and 5.

## Dissenting Opinion

*Judge Brandt, dissenting from Parts 4 and 5.*

I join Parts 1, 2, and 6 of the Court's opinion, and Part 3 except as to the waiting period, which I agree with Judge Achterberg is valid. I write separately on the Calder appeal, because I would not reach the question the Court answers.

Ruth Calder signed a written consent, which the State recorded. She did not challenge it for eight months, during which a child was born. The Constitution protects consent "given freely," and the proper time to test that is before the marriage is recorded, which is what Section 12A now does. Once a consent is recorded and a new family has relied on it, I would allow a court to set it aside only on clear and convincing evidence of violence, or of a threat of violence. A dispute about money in a failing marriage, however ugly, is for the courts that divide property and order support. Those courts can make Daniel Calder pay every penny he owes. They need not unmake a marriage to do it.

I respectfully dissent.
