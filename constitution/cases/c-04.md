# CASE — PELL v. CITY OF HARROWGATE

*Decided together with* **City of Harrowgate v. Northgate Ambulance Company** *and* **City of Harrowgate v. Vane**

**Court:** Supreme Court of Concordia, on appeal from the Court of Appeals

**Questions:** Whether a city ordinance that bans every "device that has a motor and four or more wheels" from its parks may be applied to a person using a power wheelchair, and to an ambulance crossing a park to reach a person in cardiac arrest; and whether a court may extend the same ordinance to a two-wheeled motorcycle because leaving it out would be absurd

**Result:** For Pell on the first question, for Northgate Ambulance Company on the second, and for Vane on the third. Decided 9–0 on the first question, 6–3 on the second, and 9–0 on the third.

**Provisions applied:** VIII.1.d, VIII.1.e, VIII.1.f, I.4.a, I.4.c, VI.3.a

---

## Summary

The City of Harrowgate, in the state of Kestrel, bans motor vehicles from its parks. Its ordinance defines a motor vehicle as "any device that has a motor and four or more wheels." Read as written, that definition reaches a power wheelchair and an ambulance, and it does not reach a motorcycle.

These three cases ask how the absurd results rule of VIII.1.e applies to that definition. Nadia Pell was fined for using her power wheelchair on a park footpath. Northgate Ambulance Company was fined because one of its ambulances crossed a park lawn to reach a man in cardiac arrest. Garrett Vane was fined for riding a dirt bike across the same lawns and damaging them.

The Court holds that applying the ban to Pell's wheelchair, and to an ambulance answering an emergency in the park, produces results that no reasonable person who adopted the ordinance could have intended. Both fines are cancelled. The Court also holds that leaving motorcycles out of the ban cannot be cured by a court, whether or not it is absurd. The absurd results rule may never be used to create or increase a penalty that a person would not bear under the text (VIII.1.e). Vane's fine is cancelled, and the question goes back to the City Council, which can fix the ordinance in an afternoon.

---

## Background

### The ordinance

The Harrowgate Parks Ordinance provides, in part:

> **Section 2 — Purpose.** This Ordinance keeps the city's parks safe, quiet, and open to people on foot.
>
> **Section 3 — Definitions.** "Motor vehicle" means any device that has a motor and four or more wheels.
>
> **Section 4 — Motor Vehicles.** No person shall drive or park a motor vehicle in a city park, except on a public road that passes through it. The registered owner of a motor vehicle, or where there is none, the person driving it, shall pay a fine of $150. Where the grounds are damaged, the court may add a fine of up to $1,000.
>
> **Section 6 — Permits.** The Parks Department may issue a permit for a maintenance vehicle to enter a park.

The Ordinance has no exception for emergency vehicles or for mobility devices. The city enforces Section 4 with cameras at park entrances, which issue a fine to the registered owner of any vehicle they record, and with park rangers.

### Pell

Nadia Pell has multiple sclerosis and cannot walk more than a few steps. She uses a power wheelchair with four wheels and an electric motor, which moves at about walking speed. She has used the footpaths of Linden Park, near her home, every day for six years.

After the Constitution took effect, the Parks Department told its rangers that VIII.1.d requires every provision to be "applied as written," and that a power wheelchair is a device with a motor and four wheels. A ranger stopped Pell on a footpath and fined her $150. The ranger told her that she could still use the park in a manual wheelchair, which has no motor. Pell cannot propel a manual wheelchair for more than a short distance.

The trial court upheld the fine, holding that the text was clear. The Court of Appeals reversed under VIII.1.e. The city appealed.

### Northgate Ambulance Company

On a Sunday afternoon, a man collapsed on the main lawn of Linden Park while playing football. A bystander called for an ambulance and began chest compressions. Northgate Ambulance Company, which provides emergency medical services in Harrowgate under contract with the county, sent an ambulance.

The nearest point a public road reaches is four hundred meters from where the man lay. The ambulance crew drove through a park gate and across the lawn, and reached him about three minutes sooner than they could have on foot carrying their equipment. A paved service path, which the ambulance could have used, would have brought it within one hundred and fifty meters of the man, about ninety seconds sooner than on foot. The crew restored the man's heartbeat with a defibrillator. He survived.

The entrance camera recorded the ambulance, and the city issued a $150 fine to Northgate. Northgate asked the city's Fines Office to cancel it. The Office refused, writing that "the Constitution now requires us to apply the text as written, and the text contains no exception for ambulances." The trial court cancelled the fine under VIII.1.e, the Court of Appeals affirmed, and the city appealed.

### Vane

Garrett Vane rode a two-wheeled dirt bike across the lawns of Linden Park and the cricket ground in its center, at speed, on a Saturday evening. The ride cut ruts in the cricket pitch that cost the city about $4,000 to repair. A ranger fined him $150 under Section 4, and the trial court added $800 for damage to the grounds.

Vane argued that his dirt bike has two wheels and is not a motor vehicle under Section 3. The trial court agreed that it is not within the definition as written, but held that leaving motorcycles out of the ban is an absurd result under VIII.1.e, and read the definition to include them. The Court of Appeals affirmed. Vane appealed.

---

## Questions Presented

1. Does Section 4 of the Ordinance apply to a person using a power wheelchair on a park footpath?
2. Does Section 4 apply to an ambulance crossing a park to reach a person who needs emergency care there?
3. May a court read the definition of "motor vehicle" to include a two-wheeled motorcycle, because leaving motorcycles out would be absurd?

---

## Opinion of the Court

*Judge Seo delivered the opinion of the Court. Judges Halvorsen, Mbeki, Ruiz-Tanaka, Oyelaran, and Castellanos joined the opinion in full. Judges Brandt, Achterberg, and Lindqvist joined Parts 1, 2, 4, and 5.*

### 1. How the rules fit together

VIII.1.d and VIII.1.e govern how every court and every governmental official reads every law, at every level of government. They apply to the ordinances of Harrowgate as they apply to the Constitution.

VIII.1.d comes first. Words have the meaning an ordinary, reasonable reader would have understood them to have when the provision was adopted, and a word the document defines has its defined meaning. Where the meaning is clear, it is applied as written. Only where the text can reasonably be read in more than one way do the rules for ambiguity apply.

The definition in Section 3 is not ambiguous. A power wheelchair has a motor and four wheels. So does an ambulance. A dirt bike has a motor and two wheels. No ordinary reader could understand "four or more wheels" to mean two, or "any device" to leave out devices that carry people who cannot walk. None of these cases turns on ambiguity. If the parties are to escape the text, they must do it under VIII.1.e.

VIII.1.e is a narrow exception to VIII.1.d, and it is meant to be. Three features keep it narrow.

First, it asks about the **result** of applying a provision, not about the provision in general. A provision may be sensible in nearly every case and absurd in one. The question is whether that one result is one that no reasonable person who adopted the provision could have intended.

Second, it names the grounds on which a result may be absurd, and it says what is not enough: a result is not absurd "because it is harsh, costly, unpopular, or unwise, or because the court or official would have chosen differently." A court that finds a result absurd must be able to say why the drafters could not have wanted it, not merely why the court does not.

Third, it never works in one direction. It may not be used to narrow a right, to enlarge a power of government, or to create or increase a penalty, offense, tax, fee, or duty that a person would not bear under the text. Where only such a reading would avoid the absurd result, the text applies as written until the body that made it corrects it.

The person claiming an absurd result bears the burden of showing it. In the first two cases, that is Pell and Northgate. In the third, it is the city.

### 2. The ban does not apply to Pell's wheelchair

The city argues that the Council may simply not have thought about power wheelchairs, and that a result the drafters did not foresee is not the same as one they could not have intended. That is true as far as it goes. Many results are unforeseen, and most of them are not absurd. A court may not rewrite a law because its drafters failed to imagine a case.

But applying the ban to Pell is not merely unforeseen. It defeats the Ordinance's own purpose, as shown by its text (VIII.1.e, ground 3). Section 2 states that the Ordinance keeps the parks "safe, quiet, and open to people on foot." A power wheelchair is how Pell moves in place of walking. It travels on the footpath, at walking pace, and makes less noise than a conversation. It presents none of the dangers that the ban on motor vehicles addresses. Applying the ban to her does not make the park safer or quieter for anyone. It only closes the park to a person who cannot walk, in the name of keeping it open to people on foot.

The result is also so contrary to common sense that no reasonable reader would accept it (ground 4). The ranger told Pell that she could use the park in a manual wheelchair. On the city's reading, the Ordinance permits the same person, in the same chair, on the same path, at the same speed, as long as she cannot actually move it. The line the city draws does not separate dangerous devices from safe ones. It separates people who can push themselves from people who cannot.

Pell has met her burden. VIII.1.e requires a court to adopt the reading that departs least from the text and avoids the absurd result. That reading is this: **a motor vehicle under Section 3 does not include a mobility device used, at walking pace, by a person with a disability in place of walking.** Everything else in the definition stands. The reading narrows no right, enlarges no power, and creates no penalty. It removes one.

Pell also argues that the ban, applied to her, discriminates on the basis of disability, a protected status under I.4.a, and fails the strict test of I.4.c. Because the Ordinance, correctly read, does not apply to her, we need not decide that question. We note only that if the Council were to amend the Ordinance to ban power wheelchairs expressly, the result would no longer be unintended, VIII.1.e would no longer apply, and the question under I.4.a would have to be decided. The city has not suggested any interest that such a ban would serve.

### 3. The ban does not apply to an ambulance answering an emergency in the park

The city's strongest argument concerns the ambulance. It points out that the Ordinance protects the grounds, that an ambulance is the kind of heavy vehicle the ban is plainly aimed at, and that the crew could have used the paved service path and lost only ninety seconds. A Council that wanted heavy vehicles off the lawns even in an emergency, the city argues, might have been unwise, but it would not have been absurd. And a $150 fine against a company is not the sort of harsh result that VIII.1.e reaches.

We disagree, on the first of those points and therefore on the rest. The question is not whether the crew chose the only possible route. It is whether a reasonable person who adopted an ordinance to keep the parks "safe" for the people in them could have intended to penalize an ambulance for taking the fastest route to a person dying in the park.

No reasonable person could. Every minute without defibrillation in cardiac arrest reduces a person's chance of survival substantially, and the crew had no way to know whether ninety seconds would be the difference. Section 2 states that the Ordinance exists to keep the parks safe. A fine for reaching a dying man as fast as possible punishes the conduct that most directly serves that purpose, and it does so to protect grass. That defeats the Ordinance's purpose (ground 3). The size of the fine does not change the result. A small penalty that teaches every ambulance crew in Harrowgate to stop at the edge of a park and run is not a small matter.

The city is right that harshness alone is not absurdity, and right that a court may not substitute its own judgment of what the Council should have done. If the Ordinance said, in terms, that ambulances must use the service path, we would apply it, whatever we thought of it. But the Ordinance says nothing about ambulances at all. It does not express a judgment about emergencies that we are overriding. It expresses no judgment about them, and a literal application of it to an emergency produces a result that contradicts the one judgment it does express.

The reading that departs least from the text and avoids the absurd result is this: **Section 4 does not apply to an emergency vehicle answering an emergency inside a park.** We do not hold that ambulances may cross parks whenever it is convenient, or that the city may not regulate the routes they take. A vehicle that enters a park for any other purpose is subject to Section 4 like any other.

### 4. Officials must apply VIII.1.e too

The Fines Office refused to cancel Northgate's fine because it understood VIII.1.d to require it to apply the text as written. That was a misreading of VIII.1.d, and it was not the Office's alone. VIII.1.d itself provides that clear text applies as written "except as VIII.1.e permits," and VIII.1.e binds every governmental official, not only courts.

An official who meets an absurd result may, and must, apply VIII.1.e. In doing so, the official must state in writing the result found absurd, why it is absurd, and the reading adopted, and the decision is subject to judicial review (VI.3.a). The rule gives officials no license to disregard laws they dislike, and the written reasons and judicial review are there to catch any who try. But neither does it permit an official to fine an ambulance company for saving a life and tell it that the Constitution required the fine. A person should not have to go to court to get a result that the official could see was absurd.

### 5. A court may not extend the ban to Vane's motorcycle

The trial court held that leaving two-wheeled motorcycles out of the ban is an absurd result. It may well be. A dirt bike at speed is louder, faster, and more dangerous to people on foot than most four-wheeled vehicles, and it cut ruts in the cricket ground that a car could hardly have managed. It is difficult to imagine that the Council intended the parks to be closed to golf carts and open to motorcycles. More likely, the drafter of Section 3 was thinking of cars and wrote a definition that fit them.

But we need not decide whether the result is absurd, because VIII.1.e could not cure it if it were. The only reading that would bring Vane within Section 4 is one that adds "two" wheels to a definition that says "four or more," and so creates a penalty that he would not bear under the text. VIII.1.e forbids exactly that. Where the only way to avoid an absurd result would do so, "the text applies as written until it is corrected," and for an ordinance, it is corrected by the body that made it.

The limit is not an accident of drafting, and it does not reflect a view that the people of Concordia are indifferent to damaged parks. It reflects the oldest rule of fair government: a person may be punished only for conduct that the law, as written, forbids. The same rule appears in the requirement that ambiguous penalties be read in the person's favor (VIII.1.d), in the rule against vague criminal laws (II.7.a), and in the rule against fines under provisions too unclear to understand (VIII.1.f). A court that could add to a penalty whenever it found a gap absurd would decide, after the fact, what conduct was forbidden. That is a power the Constitution gives to no court and no official.

Vane's fines under Section 4 are cancelled. Nothing in this holding limits the city's other remedies. The city may seek the cost of repairing the cricket ground from Vane under the ordinary law of damage to property, and it may charge him under any other law his conduct violated. It may not fine him under a provision that does not reach him.

---

## Disposition

In **Pell v. City of Harrowgate**, the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed. Pell's fine is cancelled.

In **City of Harrowgate v. Northgate Ambulance Company**, the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed. Northgate's fine is cancelled.

In **City of Harrowgate v. Vane**, the judgment of the Court of Appeals is reversed. Vane's fines under Section 4 are cancelled.

As VIII.1.e requires, the Clerk shall send this decision to the City Council of Harrowgate, which made the Ordinance, with the Court's findings: that Section 4, read as written, would ban power wheelchairs and ambulances answering emergencies, which the Court has held absurd; and that Section 3, read as written, does not reach two-wheeled motorcycles, a result the Court could not correct.

## Concurring Opinion

*Judge Oyelaran, joined by Judge Castellanos, concurring.*

I join the Court's opinion in full. I write about Part 2.

The Court decides Pell's case under VIII.1.e and leaves aside her claim under I.4.a. That is a reasonable course, and I join it. But it has a cost. A holding that one ordinance, correctly read, does not apply to wheelchairs protects the people of Harrowgate. It does nothing for a wheelchair user in any other city whose ordinance says the same thing, and it would do nothing for Pell if the Council chose to amend the Ordinance to say plainly what the Parks Department believed it said.

The absurd results rule is a tool for reading laws, and the Constitution limits it carefully, for good reason. It is not a substitute for the rights the Constitution protects. Where, as here, a person has both a statutory and a constitutional claim, I would expect courts to decide the statutory claim first, as the Court does, and I would expect governments to read today's decision as the Court's warning about the constitutional one. A ban on power wheelchairs in public parks would close the parks to people because of disability, and I cannot imagine the interest that would justify it.

## Concurring Opinion

*Judge Lindqvist, concurring in Parts 1, 2, 4, and 5.*

Part 5 lets a man who tore up a cricket ground escape a fine for doing it. I join it without hesitation. The limits in VIII.1.e will sometimes produce results like this one, where a court can see a gap in a law and is not permitted to fill it with a penalty. That is the price of a rule that no person may be punished for conduct the law did not forbid, and it is a small one. The gap in Section 3 will be closed by the Council, quickly and openly, which is how it should be closed. A court that closed it instead would be making law and punishing Vane under it in the same act.

## Opinion Dissenting in Part

*Judge Brandt, joined by Judges Achterberg and Lindqvist.*

I join Parts 1, 2, 4, and 5 of the Court's opinion. I respectfully dissent from Part 3.

The Court is careful to say that VIII.1.e does not reach a result merely because it is harsh or unwise. Then it applies the rule to a result that is, at most, both. The Council banned heavy vehicles from park lawns, which are soft ground covered on a Sunday afternoon with people sitting and lying on blankets. A paved service path leads to within one hundred and fifty meters of where the man lay. A Council could reasonably want ambulances to use it, at the cost of about ninety seconds, rather than drive across a crowded lawn. The fine for not doing so was $150, payable by a company.

One may think that judgment mistaken. I might think so myself. But a judgment we disagree with is not one no reasonable person could have intended, and the difference between them is the whole of the protection VIII.1.e offers against judges who would rewrite laws to their own taste. The Court says the Ordinance expresses no judgment about emergencies. It expresses a judgment about vehicles on lawns, without exception, and the Council had every opportunity to write one. If it wishes to exempt ambulances, as I expect it will, it can do so.

Pell's case is different. Applying the ban to her serves no purpose the Ordinance names, and closes the park to her entirely. That is absurd. Fining an ambulance company $150 for not using a service path is harsh, and I would leave it to the Council to decide whether it is too harsh.
