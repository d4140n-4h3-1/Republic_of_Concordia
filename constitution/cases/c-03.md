# CASE — CONCORDIA v. VOSS

**Court:** Supreme Court of Concordia, on appeal from the Court of Appeals

**Questions:** Whether a sentence of life imprisonment for a mass shooting in which no one was killed is grossly disproportionate; whether a person convicted of a mass shooting may also be punished for attempted murder of the people wounded in it; and how a sentencing court must weigh the reason a shooting failed

**Result:** For the government on the first question; for Voss on the second and third. Decided 9–0 on the sufficiency of the evidence; 7–2 upholding the life sentence; 9–0 on multiple punishment; and 6–3 on resentencing.

**Provisions applied:** II.7.b, II.4.g, II.4.f, II.8.c; Homicide Act, Sections 4, 7, 8, and 9

---

## Summary

Ellis Voss fired thirty-one rounds from a rifle into a crowd of about two thousand people at a public concert in the capital district. Two people were wounded, one seriously. No one was killed. His rifle jammed. He had two more loaded magazines, but he put the rifle down, walked to the street, and sat on the curb until officers arrested him four minutes later.

A jury convicted Voss of a mass shooting under the Homicide Act, Section 8.a, and of two counts of attempted murder, one for each person wounded. The trial court sentenced him to life imprisonment, with parole eligibility after thirty years, the latest the Act allows, and to twenty-five years for each attempted murder, to run at the same time.

The Court holds, first, that the evidence was sufficient for the jury to find that Voss intended to kill persons chosen without regard to who they were. Second, that life imprisonment for a mass shooting, even one in which no one dies, is not grossly disproportionate under II.7.b, because the Constitution measures the seriousness of an offense by risk and culpability as well as by harm. Third, that the attempted murder convictions punish the same act as the mass shooting, and cannot stand alongside it under II.4.g. Fourth, that the trial court did not weigh, as the Act requires, the evidence that Voss chose not to reload. The case is returned for the trial court to set the parole date again.

---

## Background

### The shooting

On the evening of the shooting, Riverside Plaza in the capital district held a free outdoor concert attended by about two thousand people. At 9:40 p.m., Voss fired from the fourth level of a parking garage that overlooked the plaza. He used a semi-automatic rifle that fires one shot with each pull of the trigger.

Over about ninety seconds, Voss fired thirty-one rounds. Investigators recovered bullet strikes across the width of the plaza, most between one and two meters above the ground, and a few in the trees and the stage canopy above the crowd. Two people were struck: Mira Castell, in the shoulder, and Tobias Wren, in the abdomen. Wren survived after two operations. No one was killed.

On the thirty-first round, the rifle jammed. Voss had two more loaded magazines in his jacket. Security video shows him working the rifle's bolt for about ten seconds, then laying the rifle on the floor of the garage. He walked down the stairs to the street, sat on the curb, and waited. Officers arrested him four minutes later. He did not resist.

### What Voss had said

Three days before the shooting, Voss posted on an online forum: "Saturday at Riverside. I want the highest number. Doesn't matter who." Investigators found a notebook in his home with a sketch of the garage and the plaza, and the words "fourth floor, clear line, 2000+."

At trial, Voss testified that he had changed his mind once he began firing, that he had aimed above the heads of the crowd to frighten people rather than to kill them, and that he had put the rifle down because he could not go on.

### The trial

The government charged Voss with mass shooting under the Homicide Act, Section 8.a, and with two counts of attempted murder under Section 7, one for each person wounded. The plaza and the garage are in the capital district, so the Act applies (Homicide Act, Section 3).

The jury found Voss guilty on every count. It found, in a special verdict, that he intended to kill persons gathered in a place, chosen without regard to who they were (Section 4.e(2)).

At sentencing, Wren and Castell gave statements under the Victims of Crime Act, Section 5.b. The trial court sentenced Voss to life imprisonment for the mass shooting, with parole eligibility after thirty years, and to twenty-five years for each attempted murder, all to run at the same time. On the reason the shooting ended, the court said: "He stopped because his rifle jammed. That is luck, not mercy, and it earns him nothing."

### Proceedings below

The Court of Appeals affirmed. Voss appealed on four grounds: that the evidence did not prove intent to kill; that a life sentence for a shooting in which no one died is grossly disproportionate under II.7.b; that the attempted murder sentences punish him twice for one act under II.4.g; and that the trial court failed to consider why the shooting ended, as Section 9.c(4) requires.

---

## Questions Presented

1. Was the evidence sufficient to prove that Voss intended to kill persons chosen without regard to who they were?
2. Is life imprisonment, with parole, grossly disproportionate for a mass shooting in which no one is killed?
3. May a person convicted of a mass shooting also be punished for attempted murder of the people wounded in it?
4. Did the trial court weigh the reason the shooting failed, as the Homicide Act requires?

---

## Opinion of the Court

*Judge Ruiz-Tanaka delivered the opinion of the Court. Judges Halvorsen, Mbeki, and Oyelaran joined the opinion in full. Judges Achterberg, Brandt, and Seo joined Parts 1, 2, and 3. Judges Castellanos and Lindqvist joined Parts 1, 3, and 4.*

### 1. The evidence of intent was sufficient

A mass shooting requires intent to kill (Homicide Act, Section 4.e), and the government must prove that intent beyond a reasonable doubt (II.4.f). Voss argues that he aimed above the crowd to frighten it.

The jury was entitled to reject that account. Voss announced in advance that he wanted "the highest number" and that it "doesn't matter who." He chose a position with a "clear line" over a crowd he expected to number more than two thousand. Most of his rounds struck between one and two meters above the ground, where people's heads and bodies were, and two of them struck people. A person who fires thirty-one rounds into a dense crowd at that height knows that death is practically certain to result, which is itself intent to kill under Section 4.b. The few strikes in the trees and the canopy do not show a different purpose. They show that some shots missed.

We do not reweigh the evidence. We ask whether a reasonable jury could find each element beyond a reasonable doubt. This one could.

### 2. Life imprisonment for a mass shooting is not grossly disproportionate

II.7.b forbids any punishment that is "grossly disproportionate to the offense." The Constitution defines the **seriousness of an offense** as "the legally relevant degree of harm, culpability, risk, and other circumstances associated with an offense." The question is whether life imprisonment, with the possibility of parole, is grossly out of proportion to a mass shooting measured by all of those things.

**Risk.** The definition names risk expressly. The drafters did not measure seriousness by harm alone. A person who fires thirty-one rounds into a crowd of two thousand creates a risk of death to every person in it. That risk was not remote or speculative. It was realized for two people, and it fell short of death for them only because of where the bullets struck and how quickly they reached a surgeon.

**Culpability.** Voss planned the shooting days in advance, chose a crowd for its size, and fired on it with intent to kill as many people as he could. Few states of mind are more culpable.

**Harm.** No one died, and the harm is less than it would have been if they had. But the harm was not small: two people were shot, one nearly fatally, and two thousand people fled for their lives. A shooting into a crowd also injures a community in a way an attack on one person does not. It tells everyone who goes to a public gathering that they may be next.

Voss argues that the Constitution itself ties the gravest punishment to death: a sentence of death may be imposed only for aggravated murder (II.7.e). That is true, and it shows that the Constitution treats death as a penalty apart. It does not show that the next most severe penalty, life imprisonment with a real opportunity for parole, also requires a death. Life with parole is not the death penalty. A person serving it may be released, and must be considered for release no later than thirty years into the sentence (Homicide Act, Section 9.b).

Voss also points out that the Homicide Act punishes attempted murder of one person by no more than twenty-five years. A mass shooting differs from that offense in exactly the ways II.7.b counts: it creates a risk of death to many people at once, it is aimed at people chosen without regard to who they are, and the person who commits it has done everything in their power to kill. Congress may reasonably judge that the difference between a failed attempt on one life and a failed attempt on hundreds justifies a higher maximum.

We hold that a maximum sentence of life imprisonment, with parole eligibility no later than thirty years, is not grossly disproportionate to a mass shooting, whether or not anyone is killed. Whether a particular sentence within that maximum is proportionate depends on the facts of the case, under Section 9.c and II.7.b.

### 3. The attempted murder sentences cannot stand

II.4.g provides that "no person shall receive more than one punishment for the same act or omission, unless each offense for which the person is punished requires proof of a fact that the others do not."

The Homicide Act provides that a person who kills in the course of a mass shooting may also be convicted of murder for each person killed (Section 8.c). Murder requires proof that a person died, which a mass shooting does not. Each offense requires a fact the other does not, and both may be punished.

Attempted murder is different. It requires intent to kill and a substantial step toward killing a person (Section 7). Every mass shooting proves both. A person who fires at or among people with intent to kill three or more of them, or people chosen at random, has intended to kill, and has taken far more than a substantial step toward killing, each person in the line of fire. Attempted murder requires no fact that a mass shooting does not.

The government answers that each attempted murder count named a particular victim, Castell or Wren. But the Act does not require that a victim be wounded, or named, to prove attempted murder, and the government proved Voss's intent toward Castell and Wren by exactly the same evidence that proved the mass shooting: the same rounds, fired at the same crowd, with the same purpose. The fact that two bullets struck people is relevant to sentencing for the mass shooting (Section 9.c(2)). It does not create a separate offense.

The attempted murder convictions are vacated. Congress did not say whether it intended attempted murder to be punished alongside a mass shooting, as it did for murder in Section 8.c. Even if it had, II.4.g would forbid it.

### 4. The trial court must weigh the reason the shooting failed

In setting a sentence for a mass shooting, the court "shall consider... any reason the offender failed that was within their control, such as a choice to stop firing" (Section 9.c(4)). The trial court found that Voss stopped because his rifle jammed, and said that this "earns him nothing."

The jam was not within Voss's control, and the trial court was right to give it no weight. But the record shows something else. After the jam, Voss had two loaded magazines, and the rifle could have been cleared. He tried for ten seconds, then put it down, left it, and waited to be arrested. Whether he did so because he could not clear the rifle, because he feared capture, or because he chose not to kill anyone else is a question of fact. If it was a choice, it was exactly the kind of choice Section 9.c(4) requires a court to weigh, and every person who might otherwise have been shot in the next minute is alive because of it.

The trial court did not decide that question. It treated the jam as the whole story. We do not say what weight the court must give Voss's conduct, or that his parole date must change. We hold only that the court must make the finding and weigh it. The parole date is vacated, and the case is returned for the court to set it again.

This is not abandonment under Section 7, which requires that the person abandon the attempt "before any person is harmed." Two people were harmed. Section 9.c(4) concerns sentencing, not guilt.

---

## Disposition

The conviction for mass shooting and the sentence of life imprisonment are affirmed. The convictions for attempted murder are vacated. The parole eligibility date is vacated, and the case is returned to the trial court to set it again, after finding why Voss did not reload and weighing that finding under the Homicide Act, Section 9.c(4).

---

## Concurring Opinion

*Judge Oyelaran, concurring.*

I join the Court's opinion in full. I write to say what Part 4 does not do. It does not reward a shooter for incompetence, or for the courage of the people who stop him. A jam, an empty magazine, or a bystander's tackle is chance, and chance earns nothing. What earns weight under Section 9.c(4) is a choice: the moment when a person who can go on killing decides not to. The law should want every shooter to know that such a choice counts. That is why Congress wrote the factor, and why the trial court must decide whether Voss made one.

## Opinion Concurring in Part and Dissenting in Part

*Judge Brandt, joined by Judges Seo and Achterberg as to Part B.*

**A.** I join Parts 1, 2, and 3 of the Court's opinion.

**B.** I would affirm the parole date. The trial court heard the evidence, watched the video, and saw a man work his rifle's bolt for ten seconds before giving up on it. It concluded that he stopped because his rifle failed him, not because his conscience stopped him. Its words were blunt, but its finding was clear, and it is one a court that saw the evidence was entitled to make. Section 9.c(4) requires a court to consider why the shooting failed. It does not require the court to write an essay about a possibility it has already rejected. I respectfully dissent from Part 4.

## Opinion Concurring in Part and Dissenting in Part

*Judge Castellanos, joined by Judge Lindqvist.*

I join Parts 1, 3, and 4 of the Court's opinion. I cannot join Part 2.

The law has always punished what a person does, not only what they hoped to do. That is why murder is punished more severely than attempted murder, though the murderer and the person who tries and fails may be equally wicked. The Court says that the outcome of a mass shooting is chance. So is the outcome of every attempt. If chance were a reason to punish a failed attempt as severely as a completed one, there would be no reason left to distinguish them at all.

The Constitution keeps that distinction. It reserves its gravest penalty for aggravated murder: for killing, not for trying (II.7.e). The Homicide Act keeps it too, punishing attempted murder by no more than twenty-five years. The Court answers that a mass shooting risks many lives at once. It does, and that is a reason to punish it more severely than an attempt on one life, perhaps much more. It is not a reason to punish a shooting that killed no one exactly as severely as murder.

I would hold that life imprisonment for a mass shooting is grossly disproportionate unless the shooting killed or seriously injured at least one person. Wren was seriously injured, so on these facts the sentence would stand. But the Court's holding reaches the shooter whose every round misses, and for that person I would draw the line where the Constitution draws it: at harm. I respectfully dissent from Part 2.
